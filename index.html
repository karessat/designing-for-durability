The shift

The economics of durability are changing.

Repair people use a simple rule. If the fix costs more than half the price of a new machine, and the machine is more than halfway through its life, buy a new one. If not, fix it.

For twenty-five years that rule pointed one way. The price of a new machine fell for shoppers. The price of a repair rose for them. So the thing to build was a cheap machine that ends up in the bin.

Three things are now pushing the rule the other way. New laws are removing the barriers to repair. New rules are telling machines to last longer. And for now, tariffs have put up the price of a new machine.

THE RULE REPAIR SHOPS USE

1Is the repair more than half the price of a new one?
2Is the machine more than halfway through its life?
Both yes → buy newOtherwise → fix it

A repair trade rule of thumb, not research. Typical lives, per the National Association of Home Builders: fridge 10–15 years, washer and dryer 10–13, dishwasher 9–12, range 13–15.

For the last few decades, the cost of buying a new appliance has consistently gone down. Today's major appliance purchase sets consumers back about half what it would have in 1998, in inflation adjusted dollars.

BLS major appliances CPI, divided by all-items CPI. 1998 = 100 → 49 in June 2026.

Over the same period, what households paid for repairs rose 85% after inflation.

Put the two together. Whatever share of a new machine a repair cost a household in 1998, by 2023 it cost 3.5 times that share. That share is what the repair-or-replace rule runs on.

WHAT THIS INDEX COVERSBLS publishes no index for appliance repair on its own. This one covers repair of household items, which holds three things: appliance repair, furniture reupholstery, and inside home work such as plumbing, electrical and painting. Appliance repair is one of the three.

BLS repair of household items CPI (CUUR0000SEHP04), divided by all-items CPI. 1998 = 100 → 185 in 2023. The 3.5 figure is the ratio between the two lines, which is the same before and after inflation, because both are divided by the same number.

So the rule pointed to buy new, year after year. Building a machine to last was spending money on something no customer was rewarded for choosing. Designing for the bin was the correct business decision.

TWO THINGS TO KNOWBLS marks appliance prices down when machines gain features. It does not do that to repair prices. So part of the gap between these two lines comes from how they are measured, not from what happened to households. And BLS has published the repair index only a handful of times since July 2024, after it cut part of its price collection, so 2023 is the last full year.

What it costs to build one

Steel is roughly half the weight of a washing machine. Since 1998 American steel has gone up 199%. What shoppers pay for a major appliance has gone up 1%.

Manufacturers closed that gap by building in cheaper countries, automating, and taking less per machine. Since June 2025 the first of those does less work: the 50% steel tariff is charged on the steel inside an imported appliance too.

HOW TO READ THISBoth lines show how much each has changed since 1998, not what either costs. A tonne of steel and a washing machine are not comparable prices. Steel is also one input among many, and much of this industry's production moved abroad over the period.

Drivers of Change

A convergence of factors are creating new white space for appliance OEMs.

POLITICAL / LEGAL

Right-to-repair laws are being passed

Repair bills have now been introduced in all fifty states. Eight have laws running, and Texas makes nine in September. Oregon went furthest: it banned parts pairing, where a machine's software refuses to work with a part the maker did not sell, and its law covers appliances rather than just phones and laptops.

This has not made anything easier to fix yet. Most of these laws only just came into force, and Oregon's carries no penalties until 2027. Parts networks, manuals and trained technicians take years to build. What the laws do now is take away a manufacturer's ability to stop a repair — the repairs themselves come later.

On 1 January 2026, 25.75% of Americans lived under a repair law; about 35% by this autumn. PIRG, a repair campaign group, adds up the population of states with laws in force.

ENVIRONMENTAL / LEGAL

Europe now says how long things must last

The EU has moved from telling manufacturers what to disclose to telling them what to build. Rules arrive product by product through 2030 and cover durability, repairability and recycled content, with a Digital Product Passport listing what is inside. France already prints a durability score next to the price in shops.

Electronics must be repairable from 2027 and must last from 2029. These are published dates a manufacturer can plan against.

Regulation (EU) 2024/1781 and its 2025–2030 working plan · Décret n° 2024-316

TECHNOLOGICAL

Software keeps a machine current

A machine no longer has to be replaced to improve. Firmware can add features to a washer already sitting in a kitchen, which turns a long life into something a manufacturer can earn from. The same software can also lock repairs out — which is exactly what the new laws are aimed at.

More electronics also means more to go wrong. Consumer Reports expects 31% of fridges with icemakers to have a fault by year five, and the icemaker and water dispenser are the two most common complaints. J.D. Power counted 87 problems per 100 connected appliances against 63 for unconnected ones.

Consumer Reports member surveys, 71,534 fridges bought 2015–2025 · J.D. Power 2025, about 12,755 households. Both are surveys, and the J.D. Power gap shows a link, not a proven cause.

SOCIAL

People do not trust brands, and keep the receipt

Buying is no longer the end of the relationship. People judge a brand on what happens afterwards — whether parts exist, whether anyone will come and fix it, whether the company was straight about how long it would last. That matters more when trust is low, and trust is low.

Edelman found 68% of people distrust business leaders, and the same share think those leaders deliberately mislead them. A machine you can fix is a promise a customer can check for themselves.

Edelman Trust Barometer 2025. Edelman is a PR firm; its method is published. Fieldwork Oct–Nov 2024.

ECONOMIC

The market is splitting in two

The people who buy appliances are pulling apart. Since 2023, only households earning more than $125,000 have reliably spent more once inflation is taken out. Everyone below that stalled, and the lowest earners were still climbing back to where they had started as late as mid-2024. Retailers see the same split: through 2025, sales to households above $150,000 grew by close to a fifth, while sales to households under $50,000 went flat or backwards across nearly every category.

For a mid-market brand, the lower half of that split is the customer base. Those buyers will not pay more up front for a machine that lasts longer. They will pay less for a second-hand one — and Whirlpool only sells refurbished machines that came back to it as returns.

New York Fed, May 2026, a panel of 200,000 consumers benchmarked against Census retail data · NIQ, 2026, a commercial data firm that does not publish its sample size

THIS CUTS BOTH WAYSA squeezed customer can also just buy the cheapest new machine on the shelf. Durability only reaches them two ways: costing less per year of use, or arriving second-hand and still working.
ECONOMIC

Tariffs put up the price of a new machine

Since 23 June 2025, big household appliances count as steel products for tariff purposes. Fridge-freezers, washers, dryers, dishwashers, freezers, stoves, ranges and ovens all carry a 50% tariff on the steel inside them. A washing machine holds a lot of steel.

Appliance prices jumped 6.9% between April and June 2026. The last time tariffs hit this category, in 2018, washers rose 12% — about $86 a machine — and dryers rose the same amount even though they were not tariffed.

BLS CPI · Proclamation 10896 derivative list, effective 23 June 2025 · Flaaen, Hortaçsu & Tintelnot, American Economic Review, 2020

THIS ONE MAY NOT LASTTrade policy changes with administrations. The 2018 washing-machine tariff ran for five years and expired in February 2023, and prices fell back. Treat tariffs as a push that could reverse, not a permanent change to the sums.

Proclamation 9694, extended by 10133, expired 7 February 2023

STRONG SIGNAL 01

People are already keeping their appliances longer

McKinsey asked more than 9,000 people who had actually bought a fridge, washer, dishwasher or cooker in the previous two years. In one year, the share who now wait ten years or more before replacing went from 35% to 39%. The same buyers said they care more about a machine being durable, efficient and affordable than about it being clever or connected.

McKinsey Global State of White Goods Survey, fielded Q4 2024. McKinsey is a consultancy and this is what people say, not what was measured at the till.

The same thing shows up where it is measured properly. The average American car is now 12.8 years old, a record for the eighth year in a row — and it rose again in 2024, when people bought more than 16 million new ones for the first time since 2019.

WORTH SAYING OUT LOUDThis started because of the pandemic and high prices. People were pushed into holding on before they chose to. It has carried on anyway.

STRONG SIGNAL 02

Repair law went from nothing to a third of the country in four years

These are 100 American households. In 2022 not one of them had a state law giving them the right to repair their own things.

PIRG. Hover or tap a household.

Five states went first — New York, California, Minnesota, Oregon, Colorado — covering one American in five.

Six more laws started on 1 January 2026, taking it to 25.75%. In that January alone, another 33 bills were filed across 13 states.

Connecticut joined in July. Texas joins in September, taking it past 35%.

And the law now has teeth. On 8 July 2026 the FTC and five states settled with tractor maker John Deere over keeping its diagnostic software to itself. Deere has to give owners and independent shops the same tools its own dealers have, for ten years, and pay $99 million to more than 200,000 farmers.

WHY THIS MATTERS TO AN APPLIANCE MAKERThe argument was not about tractors. It was about a manufacturer keeping repair tools inside its own service network. That describes the appliance business too.

FTC, July 2026. The order is proposed, not yet final.

STRONG SIGNAL 03

Europe set dates by which products must last

This is the part a manufacturer can put in a plan. The EU's ecodesign law arrived in July 2024 and rolls out product by product: electronics have to be repairable from 2027, have to last from 2029, and by 2030 almost everything needs a passport listing what is inside it and how to fix it.

Regulation (EU) 2024/1781; first working plan adopted April 2025

France already does it at the till. Every washing machine and television carries a durability score out of ten — covering how reliable it is, whether parts exist, what a repair costs — printed next to the price.

Décret n° 2024-316. A French government study of the earlier version, covering more than 20 million sales, found it moved online sales toward more repairable products. Manufacturers score themselves.

America arrived through a broken ice cream machine. McDonald's soft-serve machines were famously always out of order, and franchisees were not allowed to fix them because the software was locked. In October 2024 the Librarian of Congress said they could break those locks — for soft-serve machines, commercial espresso machines, fridges and ovens. iFixit and Public Knowledge asked for it; the FTC and the Justice Department backed them.

WHY THIS IS STRONG, AND WHERE IT STOPSIt is the first time repair rights crossed from consumer goods into commercial equipment, with antitrust regulators supporting it. But it only lasts three years, and it does not require any manufacturer to do anything.

Final rule 25 October 2024, in force 28 October 2024

WEAK SIGNAL 01

Two companies sell machines you can take apart

Framework sells laptops where you can swap the main board, the memory and the ports yourself. Prusa sells 3D printers as a kit — you build it — publishes the design files, keeps spare parts for years, and sells upgrade kits that turn the printer you own into the current model. It says nothing inside is glued; a screwdriver opens it.

WHY THIS IS WEAKBoth are small and neither is cheap. Prusa also held back the full designs on its newest printer before publishing them later under a new licence, so even the companies doing this are not consistent. It proves you can build and sell machines like this. It does not prove you can win a mass market with them.

Framework · Prusa Research, Open Community License, 2024–25

WEAK SIGNAL 02

Even Apple started selling repair parts

Apple, of all companies, now sells parts, tools and manuals straight to customers in 34 countries, and supplies independent repair shops that have no relationship with Apple at all.

WHY THIS IS WEAKOne company, one product type, and it followed the law rather than leading it. Apple backed California's repair bill while fighting Oregon's, so it is not a change of heart. What it shows: once repair access became law in a few places, the company most famous for blocking it built the capability once and rolled it out everywhere.

Apple Newsroom, 19 August 2025

WEAK SIGNAL 03

People queue on a Saturday to have things repaired for free

Repair Cafés are free community events where volunteers fix whatever you carry in. There are now more than 2,500 of them. Between them they run about 60,000 events a year, staffed by roughly 89,000 volunteers, and people bring them more than 1.6 million broken things.

WHY THIS IS WEAKIt is volunteers in community halls. No money changes hands, it is not a market, and it does not scale. It also has a succession problem: more than half the volunteers are over 60 and only 5% are under 25.

It shows two things at once. People do want their things mended, and will give up a Saturday morning to do it. And the skills to mend them sit mostly with pensioners — the same shortage that makes a national repair network hard to build.

Fourth Global Repair Café Survey, Counter and Quinton, University for the Creative Arts, published 3 July 2026. 946 responses from 30 countries, a 22.4% response rate.

Applied to the business

Four what-ifs for Whirlpool

Each one says what the evidence supports. Where the evidence runs out, it says that too.

PROVOCATION 01

What if appliances designed for durability are the next white space for OEMs?

Two companies have already rebuilt themselves around this. Groupe SEB — Tefal, Rowenta, Moulinex, Krups — keeps more than 90% of its electricals repairable for fifteen years, prices a spare part under a third of a new machine, and sells fixed-price repairs through 6,200 trained repairers. Caterpillar rebuilds worn parts using about 85% less material than making new ones, and wants a quarter more revenue from rebuilding by 2030.

Every one of those is money earned after the sale, and it keeps coming as long as the machine is alive.

Nobody outside Whirlpool can say whether this would work for Whirlpool, because the accounts do not break out what parts and service earn. That number already exists inside the company. It is the first thing to look at.

PROVOCATION 02

What if being first to the middle of the market could re-brand the brand?

Durability sells — but only at the top, where it is expensive. Speed Queen says it tests its home washers to 10,400 cycles, which is twenty-five years of washing, and charges $1,300 to $1,900. In the one published set of service-call data, Speed Queen and Miele both keep first-year faults under 10%. Both are small, expensive brands. Nobody is doing this at a normal price.

Whirlpool wrote down the value of the Maytag name in 2024. The filing does not say why, and write-downs happen for many reasons. But look at what is being written down: a brand whose advertising character, since 1967, has been a repairman with nothing to do because the machines never break. That is the durability position, already built, already famous, sitting inside the company best placed to use it.

PROVOCATION 03

What if a sustainable approach is now wildly profitable?

The honest answer is that nothing here proves that, and this briefing is not going to pretend otherwise. What the evidence does show is smaller and still useful.

Ask people if they would pay more for something repairable and sustainable and 59% say yes — down from 72% in 2007. Ask a narrower question about paying a green premium and 17% say yes. Both are people answering a survey, not people at a till, and numbers that far apart should not be leaned on.

The good news is that durability does not need that argument. A machine that lasts twice as long is bought once instead of twice. That is about money, not values, and it works on a customer who has never thought about waste in their life.

PROVOCATION 04

What if selling used machines could pay OEMs like Whirlpool?

Whirlpool already refurbishes appliances. Its Certified Refurbished programme cleans, repairs, tests and warrants them for a year. But read what qualifies: machines "previously returned at either an unused or lightly used condition." Those are returns and open-box units. They never really lived in anyone's house.

Car makers solved this a long time ago, and not by being better at refurbishing. They solved it with the trade-in. The dealer takes the old car at the exact moment the new one is sold, checks it against a factory standard, warrants it and sells it again. The manufacturer earns the second sale because it owns the moment the first car leaves.

Appliances have that moment too, and Whirlpool is standing in it. The delivery crew is already in the kitchen, installing the new machine and hauling the old one away. Right now that machine goes to scrap. It is a trade-in that nobody is collecting.

How ready is this?

Five questions. Two problems.

STILE asks five things about any change: do people want it, can it be built, is there a system to support it, is it allowed, and is anyone doing it. Each scores from 1 to 3. 1 means it is already normal. 3 means it is still missing. A change only really happens when all five approach 1.

Hines & McBride, University of Houston foresight programme, 2026. Scored August 2026.

Average 1.9 — on its way

Three of the five are already here. Two are not. That is a change three to five years out, not one that lands next year.

The two missing pieces are the interesting part. The law, the engineering and the customers are all in place — and none of those is an advantage, because every competitor has them at the same time.

What is missing is a repair and parts network, and a company willing to go first. Those are the two things a single company can actually build. They are not the obstacle. They are the opportunity.

What would show this thesis is wrong

  • Appliance prices falling back after the tariffs, the way they did after the 2021 spike.
  • Repair costs coming down once BLS starts publishing the numbers again.
  • Repair-law coverage stalling below 40% of Americans, or Oregon's parts-pairing penalties being pushed past 2027.
  • McKinsey's next survey showing replacement cycles shortening again.

The position

Nobody has built a mid-market brand on machines that last.

The laws have dates. The court case is decided. Customers are already keeping machines longer, and say they would rather have durable than clever. Small brands have proved it can be built.

Two things are missing: somewhere to have machines repaired, and someone willing to go first. The second one is just a decision — and the brand for it has been sitting in the portfolio since 1967.
